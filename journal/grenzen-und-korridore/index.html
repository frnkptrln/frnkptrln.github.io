Knowing the limits does not mean we can live within them.

Planetary boundaries, AI regulation, and the Viable Corridor: how do we preserve the conditions that allow us to keep acting?

Frank Peterlein 24 September 2026 Version 1 · English translation · Open for discussion

In this essay

  1. Planetary boundaries
  2. AI regulation
  3. The Viable Corridor
  4. What interventions can do
  5. The capacity to act
  6. Sources & status

Two subjects came together for me today: the new report on planetary boundaries and the call to ban artificial superintelligence. One describes the state of the systems that sustain our lives. The other seeks to prevent a new technology from escaping our control.

What interests me is a question they share: how can a system become increasingly capable of acting on the world while preserving the conditions that allow it to continue existing?

We know the boundary. Finding our way back is still a task.

The Planetary Health Check 2026 reports that seven of the nine planetary boundaries have been crossed. The extent of transgression has increased for all seven. This describes rising risks to the stability of the Earth system; it does not mean that crossing each boundary immediately triggers a tipping point or collapse. [1]

I find the discussion of natural carbon sinks particularly important. Vegetation and soils absorb some of our emissions. Heat, drought, fire, and damaged ecosystems can weaken that uptake. Our interventions therefore also put pressure on the buffers that have so far absorbed part of their consequences. [1]

Measuring a boundary does not create an institution capable of acting within it. Between a good diagnosis and an effective collective response lie competing interests, responsibilities, delays, and the question of who bears which costs. For me, this is the political core of the problem.

Which capability are we actually limiting?

On 3 September, Bernie Sanders and Greg Casar announced a proposal to permanently ban the development and deployment of artificial superintelligence. It also includes a temporary pause in advanced AI development, a new regulatory agency, and international coordination. This is an announced legislative proposal, not a ban being presented here as law already in force. [2]

My concern starts with the unit of analysis. Through companies, research groups, and other forms of organisation, people achieve capabilities that no individual possesses. AI raises a related question: are we looking at a model, a model with tools, a network of agents, or the whole arrangement of people, software, and infrastructure?

The comparison with human collectives is not a legal argument against AI legislation. It does, however, expose what a workable rule needs to specify: which capability is dangerous under which conditions of use, and where can it be effectively constrained?

Nor does the comparison imply that the risks are equivalent. Speed, ease of replication, and automated access to tools can create different requirements. That is precisely why I want to understand whether a limit is being applied at the right point in the system.

A corridor defined by conditions that must hold together

This is where my working model of the Viable Corridor comes in. It examines simplified multi-agent systems and asks how several conditions must work together for a system to remain viable: regulation of resource concentration, sufficient coupling between participants, and protection of the material basis on which the system depends. [3]

In the models studied, growing capability can put several conditions under pressure at once. Strengthening only one part is therefore insufficient in certain experimental regimes. What interests me is the arrangement of constraints as a whole: how do the power to act, a shared orientation, and the resilience of what sustains the system affect one another?

What the model supports so far These results concern constructed models, not an empirically confirmed theory of civilisation or present-day AI systems. An earlier sufficient condition was refuted in its Lorentzian continuum formulation; a corrected statement remains open. Applying the framework to these two current subjects is an interpretation here, not a proof. [3]

Planetary boundaries and political rules are different kinds of limits. The former describe scientifically identified risk zones in biophysical processes. The latter are collectively decided and must be enforced, evaluated, and, where necessary, revised. For me, the connection lies in the task of establishing a reliable relationship between diagnosis and action.

An intervention can work and still be insufficient.

My first impulse in debates like these is often: one intervention will not solve the problem anyway. A more precise version would be: its effects have to be assessed in context.

A limit can prevent a specific harm, close off a dangerous path, or buy time for better control. It can also displace risks, create new ways around a restriction, or miss an important cause. The mere existence of a rule does not tell us which of these effects will follow.

The Sanders/Casar proposal itself contains several instruments. A fair critique therefore has to ask how they work together, rather than reducing the proposal to a single prohibition switch. It would be equally unconvincing to infer from a problem's complexity that every individual intervention is useless.

A boundary becomes politically effective when we develop the capacity to stay within it.

That requires measurement, clear responsibility, enforceable interventions, and feedback about their consequences. Reserves matter too: time, resources, and institutional capacities that allow us to recognise an error while correction is still possible.

Preserving the capacity to act

From this perspective, I would approach both environmental policy and AI regulation with four questions:

  1. What specific harm are we trying to prevent, and how early can we detect its emergence?
  2. Who can actually intervene, including against the interests of powerful participants?
  3. Which other conditions come under pressure as a result of the intervention?
  4. How do we recognise that a rule is insufficient, and what correction is still possible at that point?

At this stage, the Viable Corridor is a way for me to hold these questions together. Whether the model can do more remains a question for further work. For today's connection, a more modest but demanding task is enough: to organise our capacity to act in ways that preserve its own preconditions.

We know a great deal about the limits we should not exceed. The open question is how we can act together so that this knowledge makes a difference.
